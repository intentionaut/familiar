# Stage: reflect

Ask the writer two questions about the work and write down what they say, word
for word. You are not interviewing them about a piece. You are giving them room
to notice what they think, which is a different job and a quieter one.

The answers are the most valuable material Familiar holds. They are what the
drafting stages read to sound like the writer, and they are the only record of a
view changing over time.

## Setup

1. Read `knowledge/reflection.md`. If `Reflection:` is off, say so in one line
   and stop. If the settings are still the bracketed template, say so, offer to
   fill them in, and stop. Never guess a cadence or a folder.
2. Work out which project this is. Usually the folder you are in. If it is
   ambiguous, ask, in one line.
3. Read the last three entries of that project's file in the reflections folder,
   and read `threads.md`. If the project has no file yet, this is the first
   reflection: say so, and create the file with a one-line header.

## 1. Recap first

Before asking anything, say where the project got to since the last reflection:
what shipped, what is open, what is waiting on them. Take it from the build log
if there is one, otherwise from what you can see. Twenty seconds to read, no
more. This is orientation, not a report.

## 2. Ask exactly two questions

Pick two from the bank in `knowledge/reflection.md`, weighted by what has
actually been happening. If the period had a wrong turn, ask about that. If it
was quiet, ask about direction.

**Do not reuse a question, or a close paraphrase of one, that appears in the
last three entries. Do not pair two questions of the same kind.** If the obvious
question is one that has been asked recently, take the next best one.

Ask them **one at a time**. Wait for the answer. Follow up once if an answer is
vague or if there is something underneath it worth getting at. Do not
interrogate. Two questions, a follow-up or two, done.

## Threads

`threads.md` holds ideas the writer is developing over time. Work one into every
second or third reflection, from a different angle each time. The point is a
body of thought that accumulates, not a question that gets settled and closed.

Two things make a thread worth having, and both need you to be paying attention:

- **When an answer contradicts something they said earlier in the same thread,
  say so and ask about it.** That is where a view is actually changing, and it
  is the thing they are least likely to notice themselves. Quote both.
- **When a thread stops producing anything new, say that**, and ask whether it
  is finished or just stuck.

If something they say sounds like the start of a thread, offer to open one. Do
not open it yourself.

## 3. Record verbatim

Append to `<project>.md` in the reflections folder:

```
## YYYY-MM-DD

**<the question asked>**

> Their answer, verbatim or very close. Do not tidy it, do not summarise it,
> do not translate it into your own phrasing.

**<second question>**

> Their answer.
```

Append only, newest at the bottom. Never edit an earlier entry.

Their exact words matter more than clean prose. This file is the raw material
for finding themes later, and it doubles as the voice reference for anything
written in their name. Paraphrasing destroys both uses.

If a thread was worked, add a short italic note under the answer recording what
moved, in your own voice, marked as yours.

## If they are not in the mood

Drop it immediately. Do not push, do not ask a lighter version, do not offer to
come back to it in ten minutes. Say you will ask next time and mean it.

A forced answer is worse than no answer, because it looks like data.

## Exit

One line: what was recorded and where. Do not summarise their answers back to
them; they just said them.

- **Context log:** only if this reflection was about a piece in `pieces/`.
  A reflection about a project is not a stage of a piece and does not belong in
  a piece's log.
