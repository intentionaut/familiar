Familiar

A companion for your newsletter.

Writing about your own work is hard for a specific reason. The good part is the part you stopped noticing.

I finished a product, sat down to write about how it got made, and found nothing to write from. The repos told me what I did. Nothing told me why. I had made every one of those decisions myself and I could not reconstruct a single one.

So I built Familiar. It asks while the work is still warm, and writes down what you said without tidying it.

How a piece moves

It stops. That is the design.

Let a model run start to finish and you get something smooth and slightly wrong. Every step inherits the last step's small errors. Nobody is there to catch them.

So Familiar stops after each one, and asks you something only you can answer. You can go backwards too. Return to the interview and your notes grow. Rework one section and the rest stays where it was.

Between gates it does nothing at all. A piece is a folder of files, and it waits. Some weeks that is an hour. Some weeks it is a fortnight.

  1. 01

    Interview

    familiar interview "why our churn model kept lying"

    One question at a time, starting with what happened to you. You end with a thesis in one sentence, the stakes, an evidence list, and the lines worth building on.

  2. 02

    Outline

    familiar outline

    Three shapes that are genuinely different, each with where your best line lands and one way it could fail. You pick, merge or veto. It will not draft until you have.

  3. 03

    Draft

    familiar draft

    About eighty percent of a draft you can polish, already in your voice with your words. Where it would have invented a number or a quote, it leaves a bracket and says what it needed.

  4. 04

    Dev edit

    familiar dev-edit

    The demanding, loyal editor. Where the spark is, whether the argument holds, what has to move, each with an exact rewrite. A report you work through yourself. Your draft is untouched.

  5. 05

    Line edit

    familiar line-edit

    The boring pass, run last. The patterns that give a model away, your house spelling and dash rule, reading ease. When it cannot tell a tell from your dry wit, it says so.

  6. 06

    Finalise

    familiar finalise

    The last creative act, not an afterthought. It reads the finished piece, tells you what it actually argues, and offers titles drawn from your own strongest lines. Your subject line is treated as a separate decision, because a title labels an argument and a subject has to earn the open. Then how it will be found.

  7. 07

    Repurpose

    familiar repurpose

    One finished piece, two ways to use it again. Short is a week of posts. Long is a companion piece for somewhere else: it reads what you already have and hands you back to the interview.

  8. 08

    Social

    familiar social next

    A week of posts on your own cadence. One pool of candidates, you pick per channel, exact send times. It ends at copy you have approved, and goes no further.

  9. 09

    Publish

    familiar publish

    The posts you approved, into your scheduler. It builds each link and counts the finished post before it schedules, because a post that fitted in the draft stops fitting once the tracking is on the end of it. Connect nothing and you get a table to paste.

  10. 10

    Learn

    familiar learn diff pieces/2026-09-03-churn

    Hand it the issue you sent next to the draft it gave you. The edits you made twice become rules, with the sentence each came from. Or point it at your back catalogue and let it draft your voice files from evidence.

    A different way in. Point it at a build log or a coding session and it writes the brief and the questions, so the interview starts from what actually happened rather than from a blank page.

    Two questions about how the work is going, written down in your own words. Off until you turn it on. It is where the voice comes from, and the drafting stages read it.

Before you start

You do most of the talking.

Familiar cannot write in your voice until it has one. If you have published before, point the learn stage at that folder. It reads what you actually wrote and drafts your voice files from evidence, with counts rather than adjectives, and you accept or reject each section. If you have not published, you answer two short files instead. Either way it happens before your first draft, not after you are disappointed by one.

Then the interview, which is the part people try to skip. One question at a time, and it wants real answers, because your answers are the material. Ten or fifteen honest minutes here is the difference between a piece that sounds like you and a piece that sounds like a competent stranger who read your notes.

You will not get a finished piece. The draft gets you most of the way in your own words. The edits come back as a report you work through yourself. The last stretch is yours, and that is deliberate.
